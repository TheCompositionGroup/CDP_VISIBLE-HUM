## 0. READ THIS FIRST — SHOULD YOU DOCUMENT IT AT ALL?

Sections 1 to 3 take **one evening**. They may help you sleep. They may also keep you from wasting money. Do them first.

Section 4 takes **seven nights**. You may not have that to give. Before you start it, ask yourself one thing:

> **Who will read this record?**

It needs to be a real person or office. For example:

- a neighbor who is gathering others
- a city or county code officer
- a state environmental agency
- a community contact at the facility
- an expert you plan to hire
- a lawyer
- a reporter

**Can't name one? Don't start Section 4 yet.** A log nobody reads is one more job for someone who is already tired. Find the reader first. Or keep the log just for your own peace of mind. That is a fine reason too. Just choose it on purpose.

Sending it doesn't guarantee anyone will act. This guide can help you be clear. It can't make anyone answer.

## 1. WHAT YOU CAN TRY TONIGHT

**Cost: $0. Time: one evening.**

Does the hum seem worse in bed than outside? Your room may be making it stronger.

Every room has deep pitches it boosts. These are called **room resonances**. (The room works like a pipe or a drum.)

**Step 1.** Measure the room's length, width, and height.
Use a tape measure if you have one. If not, step it off heel to toe. One shoe is about one foot.

**Step 2.** Take one measurement in feet.

**Step 3.** Divide 563 by that number. The answer is a pitch in **Hz**. (Hz, or hertz, counts pitch. A low number is a deep sound.)

**Step 4.** Repeat for the other two measurements.

Here is an example for a bedroom about 13 × 11.5 × 8.2 feet:

| Measurement | Pitch the room boosts |
|---|---|
| 13 ft | about 43 Hz |
| 11.5 ft | about 49 Hz |
| 8.2 ft | about 69 Hz |

Many bedrooms have resonances between **40 and 70 Hz**. Width and height matter too.

Deep sound also tends to be strongest at walls and in corners. Beds are often there.

**Step 5.** Pick two or three safe places to sleep. Use each one for a few nights.

**Step 6.** Each morning, write one word: **better**, **worse**, or **same**.

This changes how loud it is where you sleep. It doesn't remove the source.

**This might not work.** Real rooms are messy. Furniture and doors change things. A spot that calms one pitch can boost another. The middle of the room is not always better.

Feeling worse indoors does not prove the room is the cause. It only means the test is worth a try.

If it helps, good. Count it as one result from a few nights. Not as a promise.

*Source note: the room formula is a textbook estimate. It has not been checked against a reference. See Appendix C.*

## 2. BEFORE YOU SPEND MONEY

**Find your hum's pitch before you buy anything.** Section 4 shows how. Until then, nobody can tell you what will help. That includes this guide.

A window with two panes of glass has a weak spot. At one pitch, it stops almost nothing. The air between the panes acts like a spring. This is called the **mass-air-mass resonance**. (It is the pitch where glass-air-glass starts to shake along together.)

Here is an example. It uses two 6 mm (about 1/4-inch) panes:

| Gap between panes | Weak pitch |
|---|---|
| 2 inches (50 mm) | about 98 Hz |
| **4 inches (100 mm)** | **about 69 Hz** |
| 6 inches (150 mm) | about 57 Hz |
| 8 inches (200 mm) | about 49 Hz |

With two 1/4-inch panes and a 4-inch gap, the weak spot is near 70 Hz. Thinner glass moves it higher.

**This is a rough guide, not an answer.**

It only matters if your hum is near the weak pitch. That is usually somewhere from 40 to 80 Hz.

Is your hum somewhere else, like 120 Hz? Then this table tells you nothing about your window. It does not mean the window is fine. It means only real test data can answer.

Real windows also depend on the glass, the frame, the seals, and how it was installed. This table leaves all that out.

**Ask the seller:**

1. How big is the air gap? How thick is each pane?
2. At what pitch is this exact window weakest?
3. Can I see lab results for each pitch? These are called **transmission loss** data. (They show how much sound the window stops at each pitch.)
4. How much does it stop at **my** pitch? Not the one-number score.
5. Would sealing my old window help as much as new glass?
6. How will we check that it got better?

**No pitch-by-pitch lab results? Be careful.** A one-number score can hide a weak spot at the exact pitch that keeps you up.

*Source note: the window formula is a textbook estimate. It has not been checked against a reference. See Appendix C.*

## 3. WHY AN OFFICIAL NUMBER CAN READ LOW

Did someone test your noise and say it was low? Here is one possible reason. A low number does not, by itself, mean the test missed your hum. The test may have been taken at a different time or place, and a hum can come and go.

Most noise tests use a filter called **A-weighting**. (A filter is a rule that turns some sounds up or down before they are counted.)

A-weighting copies how ears hear everyday sound. It turns the deep pitches way down.

Here is how much it turns each pitch down. These are filter settings. They are not measurements of your hum.

| Pitch | Filter setting |
|---|---|
| 31.5 Hz | −39.4 dB |
| 63 Hz | **−26.2 dB** |
| 125 Hz | **−16.1 dB** |
| 250 Hz | −8.6 dB |
| 1000 Hz | 0.0 dB |

(dB, or decibel, is the unit for sound strength. You only need it to read this table.)

In plain words:

- The standard meter setting counts a 63 Hz hum as about **26 dB quieter** than it is.
- It counts a 125 Hz hum as about **16 dB quieter** than it is.

So a low reading can happen even when a deep hum is real. That alone does not show that a particular test missed your hum.

**This is not a trick.** A-weighting is the right tool for most noise problems. Most laws are written with it, for good reasons. It was just not made for deep hums.

Some local laws use a different filter. It is called **C-weighting**. (It turns deep sounds down much less.) Some use no filter at all.

**Find out which one your area uses.** It decides what kind of test could even help your case.

Now you know how to read a report you were given. You also know what to ask for next.

*Source note: the filter values come from a published standard (IEC 61672). We have not yet checked the exact edition and table. See Appendix C.*

## 4. THE SEVEN-NIGHT SCREENING PROTOCOL

**What this establishes:** frequency, start and stop time, duration, detected recurrence, tonal or broadband character.

**What it does not establish:** sound pressure level, the source, or whether any rule has been broken.

### 4.1 The level boundary

**Do not report a phone recording as a decibel level.** A phone microphone has unknown sensitivity, automatic gain control, and unknown low-frequency response. There is no traceable calibration behind it. An uncalibrated number carried into a meeting is the fastest way to have an otherwise sound record dismissed.

This is a limit on *your phone*, not on affordable measurement generally. A **calibrated Class 2 meter ($200–600)** can report level within its tolerance. If you own one, report level and disclose the device, method, and calibration date. If you don't, report frequency and time — which your phone does far better.

### 4.2 Recording setup

- Record **uncompressed (WAV)** where available, to avoid extra codec processing. **WAV does not mean raw** — gain control, noise suppression, and equalization may still be applied by the operating system. **[S]**
- Turn off automatic gain control, noise suppression, and voice enhancement wherever the app allows.
- Record at least **60 seconds** per sample.
- Many phone microphones filter out very low sound. If your phone shows nothing but you still hear it, that's not proof it isn't there — note it in your log.
- **Same placement every night.** Write it down: room, height above floor, distance from the nearest wall and window, orientation, case on or off, plugged in or not, microphone unobstructed, HVAC on or off.

### 4.3 Analysis

- Use a spectrum view with an FFT window of **4 seconds or longer**. A 4 s window gives **0.25 Hz** bins; 10 s gives **0.1 Hz**. **[A]**
- **Real-world accuracy will be worse than bin spacing** — probably around **1 Hz** under good conditions, and this has not been tested. **[E, OPEN]**
- Record the peak frequency, whether the peak is narrow (tonal) or a broad hump (broadband), and any harmonics.
- Free desktop audio software with a spectrum plot is sufficient.

### 4.4 Rule out your own house first

Before attributing anything outward, check for indoor and nearby sources that cycle: refrigerator, HVAC, ceiling fans, pool pump, aquarium pump, well pump, chargers and power supplies, a neighbor's equipment, road traffic, aircraft.

**Do not disable anything essential or unsafe.** Just note what is running and, where safe, compare recordings with equipment cycling on and off.

### 4.5 Wind and weather

Wind on a microphone produces low-frequency noise that can be mistaken for a tone. Log wind and weather every night. Use a foam or fur windscreen outdoors. **Flag and discard** windy nights rather than explaining them — a record that throws out its own inconvenient data is more credible than one that doesn't.

*(A wind-speed threshold for reliable outdoor recording has not been established here. TEST PROPOSED — see Appendix D.)*

### 4.6 Indoor and outdoor

Record both on the same night, within a few minutes, using the same placement discipline.

If the tone appears **more prominent indoors across repeated paired observations**, room resonance or structural transmission is worth investigating (Section 1). A single night proves nothing — the source itself may have changed between recordings. Compare the peak's prominence **within each recording**, not levels between recordings.

### 4.7 Detected recurrence

Report **"detected on 5 of 7 monitored nights."** Never "occurred on 5 of 7 nights."

You may have slept through it, missed a recording, had a phone process the signal away, or had the tone fall below what your microphone captures. **Absence of detection is not confirmed absence.**

Recurrence is still the strongest thing you can produce, because it is the one thing a single professional visit cannot give you. Illustratively: if something occurs on 5 of 7 comparable nights, one randomly chosen night has a **29%** chance of missing it. **[A — illustrative only; consultants may measure multiple nights]**

## 5. WHAT YOUR FREQUENCY MIGHT MEAN

Every branch below is **conditional and non-attributing**. A frequency is a lead. It is not an identification.

**Near 120 Hz (and 240 / 360 Hz).** Electrical transformer cores vibrate at **twice** the supply frequency through magnetostriction — **120 Hz** in the United States, with harmonics at 240 and 360 Hz. A stable tone at these frequencies is **consistent with** electrical equipment, but other equipment also produces them, and frequency alone cannot identify a source. **[S — verify]**

**40–80 Hz.** The room and window screening in Sections 1 and 2 may be relevant here. Investigate both.

**Variable, in the hundreds of hertz.** Fans and rotating machinery produce blade-passing tones at RPM/60 × blade count, which shift with load and speed. A tone that wanders may indicate rotating equipment. **[S]**

**Broad hump rather than a peak.** Broadband noise, not a tone. Different problem, different treatment, and most ordinances handle it better already.

**A useful stability test:** grid-locked tones hold their frequency almost perfectly. Machinery tones drift with load, temperature, and time of day. Whether your tone stays put across seven nights is informative — and frequency stability is the thing a phone measures best.

## 6. WHAT TO DO WITH THE RESULT

**Your record says:** *"A tone centered near 119 Hz was detected from approximately 12:20 a.m. to 5:00 a.m. on 5 of 7 monitored nights."*

**It does not say:** what caused it, how loud it was, or that anything is out of compliance.

Language that keeps you credible: *detected*, *consistent with*, *worth investigating*, *screening only*. Language to avoid: *proves*, *caused by*, *in violation*, *measured at X dB*.

**Possible next steps:** *(Who can act, what to ask each one, and what varies by state: [`who-to-send-it-to.md`](who-to-send-it-to.md). A fill-in cover letter: [`../log/cover-letter.md`](../log/cover-letter.md).)*

- **Neighbors** — do others detect the same frequency at the same hours? Multiple independent records are far stronger than one.
- **The facility or operator** — a specific frequency and time window is a far more actionable report than "there's a hum."
- **Local authority** — first ask which weighting their ordinance uses (Section 3). It determines whether their measurement would capture your problem at all.
- **Acoustical consultant** — your seven nights tell them when to measure. That can shorten an expensive engagement considerably.
- **Contractor** — the questions in Section 2, with your measured frequency filled in.
- **Yourself** — the Section 1 experiment, which needs no one's permission.

**Keep your evidence reproducible.** Store the spectrum images, timestamps, and log. Keep original audio only for events someone may need to independently review, stored locally and encrypted. Tell your household you are recording, avoid placements that capture neighbors' conversations, and delete what you don't need.

## APPENDIX A — WHY THIS GUIDE EXISTS

This began as an attempt to convert the noise into electricity. That failed, and the failure produced everything above.

Acoustic intensity: `I = 10^(dB/10) × 10⁻¹² W/m²` **[A]**

| Location | SPL | Incident intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Residential fenceline | 50 dB | 0.1 µW/m² |
| Property line | 65 dB | 3.2 µW/m² |
| Louver face | 85 dB | 316 µW/m² |
| Fan face | 100 dB | 10 mW/m² |

An impossible ceiling — 20,000 m² of surface at 100 dB, captured at **100%** — yields **200 W** against a hypothetical **1 GW** facility. At a generous 1% conversion, roughly **2 W**: nearly **nine orders of magnitude** below the load. **[A/E]**

> **The hum can be overwhelming to a person and nearly invisible to a power meter.**

Human hearing detects picowatts. **Noise is a perception problem, not an energy problem** — which is precisely why it is so hard to make anyone care about it.

**And then the finding that mattered.** Five conversion pathways were worked: piezoelectric louvers, a cooling water loop, a resonator field, a distributed resonant wall, machine-mounted harvesting. Every one that closes technically delivers its value to the **operator** — predictive maintenance, event capture, compliance.

The two outputs that help the **resident** — this documentation protocol, and a passive visualizer — produce **no power at all**.

> **The harm cannot be redeemed. It can only be measured, absorbed, or paid for — and every technical escape from that relocates the burden to the party with less standing to object.**

That is why this document is a guide and not a generator.

|---|---|

**It is not an instrument.** It establishes no level, no source, no duration, no violation. It is a sculpture whose only job is to make a tone visible in a room full of people who have been told it isn't there. Visibility and wind rejection are untested. **OPEN.**
