# Who reads a hum log

> ## ⚠ Unverified seed — no field trial yet
> Nobody has tested this page. Offices, powers, and rules differ by place. **Check each one before you rely on it.** This is not legal advice.
>
> **We don't measure how loud your hum is in decibels (dB). A phone can't do that honestly.**

A log does nothing until a person reads it. This page helps you pick that person. A cover letter you can copy is at [`log/cover-letter.md`](../log/cover-letter.md).

Written for the **United States**. Notes marked **VARIES** change by state, county, or city.

---

## Step 1. Pick one first reader

Don't send it to everyone at once. Pick one office that can act. Send it there. Then move on if they can't help.

Not sure? Start with **your city or county noise office**. If the hum seems to come from a plant or power equipment, add that owner.

---

## Step 2. Who can do what

| Who | What they may be able to do | What to ask for | VARIES |
|---|---|---|---|
| **City or county code enforcement** (sometimes "noise office," "zoning," or "police non-emergency") | Enforce the local noise law. Some can measure sound themselves. | The name of the office that takes noise complaints. A complaint number. Which filter their law uses (A or C). A night visit. | Many places have no low-frequency rule. Some use "plainly audible" instead of a number. Some only act on police calls. |
| **Electric utility** | Check their own equipment, like transformers and substations. | A written reply. A work order or ticket number. Whether any equipment near you was changed. | Private utilities, co-ops, and city-owned utilities answer to different bodies. |
| **State utility commission** (also called "public service commission") | Take complaints about a regulated utility that won't respond. | How to file. What they need from you. | Not every utility is regulated by the state. Rules differ. |
| **Facility owner or operator** (data center, factory, plant) | Fix, move, or adjust their equipment. | The community contact. A copy of their noise complaint log. Whether their permits set any noise limits. | Many have a "contact us" page or a community liaison. |
| **State environmental agency** | In a few states, run a noise program. Or check permits a facility holds. | Whether the state has a noise rule. Whether this facility has a state permit. Who to ask. | **Many states have no noise program.** The federal government stopped funding its noise office in 1982 and left most noise work to states and cities ([EPA history](https://epa.gov/history/epa-history-noise-and-noise-control-act)). Check locally before you expect help. |
| **Local health department** | In some places, treat noise as a nuisance or health issue. | Whether they take noise complaints. | Often not their job. Ask first. |
| **Elected official** (city council, county commission, state legislator) | Push an office to answer. Find the right person for you. | A named staff contact. A reply in writing. A call to the right department. | Most have staff for constituent help. Start with your own district's office. |
| **Neighbors** | Add more records. Many records beat one. | Whether they hear the same pitch at the same hours. | — |

*Table is a starting guide. We have not checked each row against law or practice anywhere. See [`docs/open-items.md`](open-items.md), item 9.*

## Step 3. How to find the right office

1. Search the web for your city or county name plus "noise ordinance."
2. Many local codes are posted online. Look for words like *noise*, *sound level*, *plainly audible*, *C-weighted*, or *low frequency*.
3. Call the main number of your city or county. Say: *"Who takes complaints about noise at night?"*
4. Write down the name, date, and what they told you.

---

## Step 4. Send it so it counts

1. Use the [cover letter](../log/cover-letter.md). Keep it to one page.
2. Attach the one-page summary and the nightly log from [`log/log-sheet.md`](../log/log-sheet.md).
3. Send by email if you can. Also keep a paper copy. Email leaves a date on the record.
4. Ask for a reply **in writing**. Ask for a ticket or case number.
5. Keep a copy of everything you send and everything you get.
6. If you hear nothing in about two weeks, write back once. Then try your elected official's office. *(Two weeks is our suggestion. Some offices have their own time limits.)*

---

## What to say, and what to leave out

**Say:** *detected*, *consistent with*, *worth checking*, *screening only*.

**Don't say:** *proves*, *caused by*, *in violation*, or any loudness number. A phone can't give one you can trust. One bad number can make a reader ignore the rest.

Don't go onto property that isn't yours. Don't confront staff. Don't post accusations. Stay calm and factual. Your records do the work.

---

## What changes by state

These change where you live. Check them before you lean on this page:

- Whether the state has any noise law or program
- Whether your city or county sets its own noise limits, and which filter they use
- Whether low-frequency or "tonal" sound has its own rule
- Which agency handles complaints about a power company
- Whether a facility's permit has noise terms
- Your right to see public records, like a facility's permit or a complaint log. Most states have a **public records law**. The name and the steps differ.
- Whether recording sound in your own home is limited. Rules on recording **people** vary by state. Keep your phone away from conversations.
